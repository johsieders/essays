## 1. At the Speed of Earthworks

The knights who landed on the Wexford coast in 1169 brought with them, in their baggage and their heads, every single thing that Chapter 1 said Ireland had done without. The castle. The charter. The county and the sheriff. The written survey, the money rent, the borough with its market and its royal grant. Behind all of it stood an idea so foreign to Ireland that the language had no word for it: that authority is a thing exercised over a defined patch of ground, by an officer who holds it from a king, and that it is written down.

None of it was any use on its own. What made the difference was the way the parts geared together. A castle took ground; the ground was surveyed into a manor; the manor paid rents in coin; the rents paid for the next castle. Each turn of the crank financed the following one, and the apparatus had been assembled and tested in Wales, on a Celtic frontier, before it was ever pointed at Ireland. The men who brought it over were the second and third sons of the Welsh March, and they were not improvising.

Against this, Ireland fielded a mosaic: a hundred and fifty kingdoms bound by clientship and hostage, a warfare of cattle-raid and honour, a landscape without a single stone castle to besiege. The result was not close. Within two years the newcomers held Dublin, Wexford, and Waterford; within twenty they were established across Leinster, east Munster, and the Meath midlands; within seventy they had reached the far side of the island, and a de Burgh sat in Connacht.

It is worth being exact about why the conquest went so fast, because the easy answer is wrong. It was not that the Irish could not fight. They could, and did, and Norman knights died in Ireland in respectable numbers. It was that they could not fight *this*. The mailed and mounted knight was, in the twelfth century, the most expensive and most effective instrument of violence in Europe, and Ireland had nothing to answer him with; the Welsh archers who came with him outranged anything on the field. But the decisive weapon was not the knight. It was the castle.

An earth mound with a timber tower on top, a ditch, a palisade — the motte-and-bailey could be thrown up in a fortnight by conscripted labour, and once it stood, it could not be taken by an army that had no engines, no sappers, and no tradition of siege. And it changed the meaning of a victory. Under the old rules, a defeated lord retired, regrouped, and came back next season; the raid was an event, not a conquest, and the land could not be *held* in the way the Normans meant. The castle held it. Each one planted at a ford or a river mouth or a pass converted a battle won into ground taken, permanently, and made the next castle possible. The Normans did not sweep across Ireland. They walked across it, at the speed of earthworks.

And yet the mosaic did what a mosaic does, which is absorb. To the Irish kings of 1170 the newcomers were not the vanguard of anything; they were another set of players, formidable ones, to be fought or hired or married as circumstances suggested. Diarmait Mac Murchada had brought them in precisely as hired help, and others followed his example. Some kings fought and were destroyed. Some submitted, kept their lands as the king of England's men, and lost little that mattered to them. Some watched their neighbours go under and calculated. There was no Irish resistance, because there was no Ireland to resist: there was a competitive political world doing what it had always done, with one new and unusually effective competitor in the game.

Henry II crossed in 1171 with an army, and it is important to be clear about why, because the reason has nothing to do with Ireland. Richard de Clare — Strongbow — had married Diarmait's daughter, inherited Leinster on his father-in-law's death, and was on the way to becoming an independent Norman king on the flank of the English realm. It had happened before, in Sicily and in Antioch, and Henry had no intention of watching it happen again eight hours' sail from Bristol. He came to Ireland to prevent a rival, not to acquire a country. That the Irish kings then came in and submitted to him — most of them, without a fight, having concluded that a distant overlord was a cheap way to be rid of a nearby one — was a windfall he had not gone looking for.

A word about who these people were, since the chapter will change its name for them. The men of 1169 were Normans, or more precisely Cambro-Normans: a French-speaking aristocracy of the Welsh March, one or two generations off the conquest of England, and heavily intermarried with the Welsh. They came with Welsh archers and Flemish settlers at their backs. Within three generations that mixture had fused, and it called itself something new — *the English of Ireland* — and so shall we. The reader should notice the mutation, because it will not be the last. The men who arrive as Normans will become English; and their great-grandchildren, to the horror of the Dublin parliament, will start becoming Irish.

What Henry took away was a title — Lord of Ireland, granted him by a compliant papacy — and a submission. What he left behind was a colony that no one had designed.

## 2. The Colony at High Tide

Here we must correct a picture, and the correction is the whole foundation of the chapter. It is natural, knowing how this ends, to imagine the medieval English lordship of Ireland as a beleaguered garrison — a few men holding a wall against the dark, doomed from the start, waiting out the centuries until the Gael took it all back. That is not what it was. For the better part of a hundred and fifty years the colony was a functioning, profitable, growing provincial society, and if you had visited it in 1290 you would have found nothing in the least beleaguered about it.

### The apparatus

The English brought the state, and in Ireland they built one from scratch. There was a justiciar in Dublin governing in the king's name. There was an exchequer taking accounts by 1200 and a chancery issuing writs. The island was divided into counties — Dublin, Louth, Kildare, Meath, Carlow, Waterford, Cork, Kerry, Limerick, Tipperary, Connacht — each with a sheriff, each with a court, each answering to Dublin, which answered to Westminster. There was a common law, applied in royal courts, with juries and assizes and the whole machinery of writ and plea. By the 1290s there was a parliament, with lords and commons and, in time, elected representatives from the shires and towns.

None of this had existed in Ireland before 1169. All of it exists in Ireland today. The lordship is the origin of the Irish state as an administrative fact, and the descent from the justiciar's court in thirteenth-century Dublin to the courts of the modern Republic is real and unbroken. It is one of the more disorienting things about the period and it should be allowed to disorient.

### The economy, which is the real story

The apparatus is easy to describe and can be over-weighted, because a chancery is only as real as the wealth that pays for it. The deeper transformation was in the soil.

The Normans brought the **manor**. The manorial economy — a demesne farm worked partly by the labour services of unfree tenants, ringed by tenant holdings paying rent in money and kind, with a mill, a court, and a church — was the standard economic organism of medieval western Europe, and it was as foreign to Gaelic Ireland as the sheriff. Gaelic agriculture was pastoral before it was arable, dispersed rather than nucleated, and reckoned in cattle. Wealth was livestock, and livestock walked; the raid was possible because the wealth had legs. The manor put wealth in the ground and in the granary, where it stayed, and where it could be counted and taxed.

Across Leinster, Meath, and the river valleys of Munster, the settlers laid this organism over the landscape: nucleated villages with open fields, arable where there had been pasture and woodland, a parish church and a mill and a court on every manor of consequence. Behind the manor came the **town**. The Normans found the Viking ports and made them properly urban — walls, charters, guilds, burgage plots — and then founded new ones by the score, chartered boroughs with markets and fairs planted deliberately as instruments of settlement. New Ross, Drogheda, Kilkenny, Galway, Youghal, Athenry: the medieval urban map of Ireland is very largely a Norman creation laid over a Norse foundation, and it is the map we still have.

The point of all this was **export**, and this is the fact that most reliably surprises. Thirteenth-century Ireland was a grain-exporting country. Wheat and oats from Leinster and Meath went out through the ports to feed the English armies in Wales, in Scotland, and later in Gascony; hides and wool and fish went to Bristol and Chester and beyond. Coin circulated — Ireland had its own mints — and the rents came in cash, and the profits flowed east. The colony paid for itself and then some. In the reign of Edward I the Irish revenue was a real contributor to English royal finance, and Irish grain and Irish soldiers helped conquer Wales. The lordship was, in the plainest economic sense, a **going concern**, and it was going concernedly for a long time.

The figures survive, and they are worth pausing over, because they let us watch the colony rise and then fall in a single column of numbers. <cite index="4-1">Between 1278 and 1299 the Irish exchequer's receipts averaged something in the region of £6,300 a year; in 1296, the year an Irish army sailed to fight for Edward I in Scotland, the recorded total was about £6,400.</cite> Then the curve turns. <cite index="4-1">In the last part-year of Edward I's reign the receipt was a little over £5,700, and in the first year of Edward II it fell below £3,000</cite> — a collapse of more than half, and this *before* the Bruces, before the famine, and before the plague. The lordship's fiscal peak was already behind it in 1307, and nobody in Dublin knew.

Two cautions, both of which the reader is entitled to. These are recorded receipts at the exchequer, not the wealth of the country: much revenue never reached Dublin at all, having been alienated to municipalities or granted away or simply stolen, and the surviving rolls are incomplete. And the historians who assembled the series — Richardson and Sayles, whose figures these are — were arguing about *when* the decline began, which is a question the numbers do not settle by themselves. Treat them as a shape, not a measurement.

And note what this does to the picture of two nations. It puts them in the same economy. Gaelic lords sold cattle into colonial markets; Gaelic labour worked colonial land; the Irish of the mountains and the English of the plains traded across a line that was, commercially, no line at all. Whatever the two nations were, they were not two economies.

### The two laws

Yet the line existed, and it was drawn in law, and this is where the two nations were actually made.

The settlers had the common law: royal courts, writs, juries, the king's peace. The Gaelic Irish had brehon law, and kept it, and it governed them in their own lordships as it had for a thousand years. Two legal systems, side by side on one island. Considered abstractly, this is unremarkable; medieval Europe was full of overlapping jurisdictions and personal laws.

But it was not abstract, because of one asymmetry, and the asymmetry is the thing to hold onto. The common law of the lordship regarded the Gaelic Irish as **outside the king's peace**. In practice this meant three things, and each is worse than the last. An Irishman could not bring a plea in a royal court; his access to the king's justice was, with narrow exceptions, simply barred. His testimony did not count as an Englishman's did. And — the sharpest edge — the killing of an Irishman was not a felony. It was not murder in the king's eyes. The killer might owe compensation to the dead man's *lord*, as one might for a beast; he did not answer to the crown for a homicide.

There were exceptions, and they prove the shape of the rule. Five Gaelic kindreds, the *quinque sanguines*, held the privilege of English law by ancient grant. Individual Irishmen could purchase a charter of denizenship, buying their way into legal personhood — and the fact that such charters were *bought*, and were worth buying, tells you exactly what was being sold. And in the 1270s and again later, the Gaelic community petitioned, offering the crown large sums, to have English law extended to the Irish generally. The petitions failed. They failed because the colonists opposed them, and the colonists opposed them because the disability was **profitable**: a man you cannot be prosecuted for killing is a man whose land is easier to take.

This is where a chapter must be careful, and where the two poles of the historiography pull hardest. The revisionist instinct is to contextualise: medieval Europe was everywhere a patchwork of legal statuses, unfree villeins in England had poor access to royal courts too, and the exclusion of the Irish was a legal-jurisdictional fact before it was a racial one. All of that is true. The nationalist instinct is to name what it was: a colonial legal order in which the native was, by law, worth less than the settler, and could be killed with impunity. That is also true, and the contextualising does not dissolve it. The honest thing is to state both and let them stand together, because they do stand together, and because the reader is perfectly capable of holding two true things at once.

Gerald of Wales had given the colony its ideology before the ink was dry on the conquest. His *Topography of Ireland*, written in the 1180s by a Norman-Welsh cleric who had visited briefly and disliked what he saw, described the Irish as a filthy, treacherous, bestial people, barely Christian, living like animals — and, conveniently, as a people whose conquest was therefore a service to God and civilisation. It is worthless as evidence about the Irish. It is priceless as evidence about the conquerors, and it is the fountainhead of a caricature that runs, in an unbroken line and often in strikingly similar words, from the twelfth century to the Victorian cartoons and beyond. Read the *Topography* and then read *Punch* in the 1860s, and the continuity is not a family resemblance. It is a transcription.

## 3. The Turn

Something happened between about 1315 and 1350, and the colony never recovered from it. What happened was not one thing. It was three things, arriving together, and the temptation to rank them should be resisted, because the sources will not support a ranking and because the convergence is the point.

### The Bruce invasion

In 1315, Edward Bruce landed in Ulster with a Scottish army. His brother Robert had destroyed an English army at Bannockburn the year before, and the Bruce strategy was now to open a second front and, if possible, to detach Ireland from the English crown altogether. Edward was crowned King of Ireland in 1316. He was joined by a substantial number of Gaelic lords, most importantly the O'Neills. For three years he marched up and down the country, from Ulster to the walls of Dublin to the Munster plains, and for three years the colony's armies failed to stop him.

He was finally killed at Faughart in 1318, and the invasion failed. But the damage was done and it was of two kinds. The material damage was ruinous: three years of campaigning, in a period of famine, with an army that lived off a countryside it did not intend to keep, left the richest parts of the colony wrecked, the manors burnt, the plough teams eaten. The moral damage was worse. The colony had been shown to be beatable. For three years, a hostile army had moved through the lordship at will, and Dublin had been unable to prevent it. Nothing about the balance of forces in Ireland was ever quite the same afterward.

And the Bruce invasion produced a document. In 1317, Domhnall Ó Néill, king of Tír Eoghain, and a coalition of Gaelic lords sent to Pope John XXII a letter — the **Remonstrance of the Irish Princes** — setting out the case against English rule in Ireland. It rehearses the conquest, the papal grant that licensed it, and the promises made and broken. It states, in detail and with heat, that the English deny the Irish access to their courts; that they hold the killing of an Irishman to be no crime; that the settlers, far from bringing the civilisation they promised the pope, have brought only dispossession. And it draws the conclusion: the papal grant is void, the allegiance is dissolved, and the Irish have transferred it to Edward Bruce.

The Remonstrance is a propaganda document produced in the middle of a war by a party seeking papal cover, and it should be read as one. It is also, in 1317, an articulate, evidenced, and coherent statement of a case that the nineteenth century would believe it had invented. Six hundred years before the Proclamation, the argument is already fully formed: that the conquest was fraudulent in its origin, brutal in its execution, and legally void in its consequence.

On the legal disability described in §2, the Remonstrance is worth hearing in its own voice, because it says from the inside what the court rolls say from the outside. <cite index="24-1">It states that in the king's courts in Ireland any non-Irishman may bring an Irishman to law on any cause whatever, while every Irishman, cleric or lay, is refused all recourse to law for the sole reason of being Irish.</cite> <cite index="24-1">It then charges that when an Englishman kills an Irishman — however noble, however inoffensive — the court inflicts no punishment; and that the more eminent the murdered man, the more his killer is honoured for it.</cite> And it reaches for the sharpest thing it can find, which is an accusation not of cruelty but of *heresy*: that the colonists teach <cite index="24-1">that it is no more sin to kill an Irishman than a dog or any other brute</cite>, and that their monks, having killed one, would not on that account miss a day's mass.

The rhetorical strategy is exact, and it is aimed at a pope. The complaint is not that the English are unkind. It is that they have placed themselves outside Christendom — that a people licensed by Rome to civilise Ireland have arrived preaching a doctrine no Christian may hold. Gerald of Wales had told the papacy that the Irish were barely Christian and wanted conquering. A hundred and thirty years later the Irish sent the papacy the same accusation, in the same language, pointed the other way.

### The plague, and the accident of geography

In 1348 the Black Death reached Ireland. It came in through the ports, as it came in everywhere, and here geography did something with consequences no one intended.

The plague fell hardest where people were dense, and in Ireland the dense places were the colony. The walled towns, the nucleated villages, the manors with their clustered cottages — this was the settlement pattern the Normans had brought, and it was the settlement pattern the plague was built for. The Gaelic world was pastoral, dispersed, thinly settled, and up in the hills and the woods and the bogs, and it suffered — the annals record it plainly — but it suffered less, and it suffered later, and it suffered in a way that did not gut an economic system.

The colony's economic system was gutted. The manorial economy of the lordship required tenants, and the tenants were dead. Rents collapsed; demesnes went out of cultivation; villages were abandoned outright, and the archaeologists find them still, the deserted medieval villages of Leinster and Meath, grass over the house-plots. And the flow of settlers from England, which had been thinning for decades, stopped altogether — because after 1350 there was land and there were wages to be had in England, and no reason on earth to cross the sea for either.

The numbers must be handled with care, and the care is part of the argument. <cite index="15-1">Accurate figures for medieval Ireland are not to be had; the usual estimate is that the towns lost somewhere between 45 and 50 per cent of their people, with the rural hinterland behind them devastated and short of labour for generations.</cite> <cite index="13-1">For the island as a whole the range offered runs from a third to a half.</cite> These are estimates built on inference — on chroniclers, on abandoned holdings, on rent rolls that stop — and not on anything one could call a count.

The differential is on firmer ground, though it too is inferred rather than measured. <cite index="14-1">The plague came in through the ports and did its worst along the eastern and southern coasts, in Drogheda, Dublin, New Ross, Waterford, Youghal, Cork; it reached Louth, Meath and Kildare before the end of 1348 and Kilkenny by Christmas.</cite> <cite index="15-1">The Gaelic Irish, living dispersed across a rural landscape rather than packed into walled towns, were less affected — though not spared, and their annals name the clerics and noblemen who died.</cite> <cite index="18-1">Ulster is recorded stricken in 1349, Connacht the same year, and the disease was still working in Mayo in 1350.</cite> No one should say the Gaelic world escaped. What can be said is that the plague fell hardest on exactly the settlement pattern the colony had built, and lightest on the one it had failed to replace.

So a bacterium redistributed power in Ireland. There is no intention anywhere in this, no agency, no plan — a flea on a rat on a ship, and a settlement pattern that made the colony an ideal host and the Gaelic countryside a poor one. It is worth sitting with, because it is a useful antidote to the whole vocabulary of resistance and reconquest and national revival, which will presently be applied to what happened next.

### The absentee crown

And England looked away. From 1337 the Hundred Years' War consumed English money, English attention, and English soldiers, and it consumed them for a hundred and sixteen years. Ireland got what was left, which was little.

Worse, the great lords of the colony increasingly did not live in it. The de Burgh inheritance passed by marriage to the Mortimers, who were English magnates with English concerns; the earldom of Ulster became an absentee's title. The lordship of Meath, the great Marshal lands — the pattern repeats. The men with the largest stake in defending the colony were in England, drawing the rents and neglecting the ground, and when the crown wanted money from Ireland it took it and sent nothing back. By the middle of the fourteenth century the Irish exchequer, which had once shipped a surplus to Westminster, was running a deficit and Westminster was making it up — grudgingly, inadequately, and with an increasingly explicit sense that Ireland was a nuisance rather than an asset.

Three things, then: an invasion, a plague, and a war somewhere else. None of them was aimed at the outcome they produced. Together they broke the machine.

## 4. The Gaelic Recovery and the Middle Nation

What follows is usually called the Gaelic resurgence or the Gaelic revival, and both terms are misleading in the same way. They suggest a return — a thing coming back, an old order reasserting itself, Ireland becoming again what it had been. That is not what happened. What expanded across Ireland after 1350 was not the Gaelic world of 1160. It was something new, harder, and considerably more dangerous, and it had learned a great deal from the men it was pushing back.

### The war-band

The Gaelic lordship of the fifteenth century differed from the kingship of the twelfth in ways that the old vocabulary of *túath* and *rí* conceals. Sacral kingship, with its inauguration rites and its ancient legitimacies, gave way to something more like a warlord's chieftaincy — a lordship whose claim rested on force and whose business was the extraction of tribute from a territory. Two innovations made it possible, and both are worth naming precisely, because both will be central to the Tudor confrontation in the next chapter.

The first is the **gallowglass**. From the late thirteenth century, Gaelic lords began hiring heavy infantry from the Hebrides and the Scottish west — Norse-Gaelic mercenaries in mail, fighting with the long axe, who came in companies under their own captains and stayed. Whole kindreds of them settled in Ireland: MacSweeney, MacDonnell, MacCabe, MacSheehy. What they gave the Irish lord, for the first time in the island's history, was a **standing professional force** that was not a levy of his clients and did not go home at harvest. It answered the Norman knight. It made the Gaelic lord a military entrepreneur.

The second is **coign and livery**, and it is the fiscal solution to the problem the first one created. A standing army must be paid, and the Gaelic lord had no exchequer, no coinage worth the name, no tax. So he billeted the troops on the country: the lord's soldiers were quartered on his tenants, who fed and housed them at their own cost, and the lord's horses along with them. It is a tax collected in kind, at sword-point, at the door. It worked. It also meant that the lord's power over his own people was now backed by an armed force living among them, and it meant that a Gaelic lordship could sustain war more or less indefinitely, at a cost paid by people who could not refuse. The Tudors will find this practice intolerable, and they will be right to, and their outrage will be entirely genuine and entirely self-serving at once.

*[<< Simms is the authority here and the argument is hers. From Kings to Warlords is the book that dismantles the idea of an unchanging Gaelic order patiently outlasting the invader. The Gaelic world of 1450 is not a survival. It is an adaptation, and a militarised one. >>]*

So the lords expanded. O'Neill and O'Donnell in the north, O'Brien in Munster, the MacMurrough Kavanaghs in the Leinster mountains at the very door of the Pale, and behind them a hundred lesser men doing the same thing on a smaller scale. They took land back. They took the abandoned manors and grazed cattle where the wheat had been. And they levied, on the colonists who remained, the **black rent**: an annual payment made by an English town or an English lordship to a Gaelic lord, in exchange for not being raided. Dublin itself paid black rent to MacMurrough. Read that sentence again. The capital of the king of England's lordship of Ireland was paying protection money to an Irish king, and entering it in the accounts.

### Degeneracy

Meanwhile, the colonists were becoming Irish, and their government was legislating against it, and the legislation is the best evidence we have that it was happening.

A word first, because we will need it and because the modern ear mishears it. *Degeneracy* is the word the sources use, and it is not, in the fourteenth century, an insult about morals or a claim about blood. *Degenerare* is Latin and literal: *de-* and *genus*, to depart from one's stock, to fall away from the kind one was born to. To call an English settler in Ireland degenerate was not to say he had become wicked. It was to say he had **stopped being English** — and that, for a colony whose entire legal and political existence rested on its people being the king's English subjects in Ireland, was not a moral complaint. It was a constitutional emergency. The word carries the exact freight the Dublin administration intended it to carry, and it will be used here without apology or quotation marks.

What it named, we would call **gaelicisation**, and by 1350 it was everywhere. The great colonial houses — the Fitzgeralds of Kildare and Desmond, the Butlers of Ormond, the Burkes of Connacht — spoke Irish, and in many cases spoke it first. They married Irish women and fostered their sons in Irish households, which was the deepest bond Gaelic society knew and created a kinship stronger than blood. They kept Irish poets and paid them for praise-poems in Irish, in the correct classical metres, on the correct hereditary terms. They took Irish law, or as much of it as suited them, and settled disputes by brehon arbitration rather than by writ. They dressed Irish, rode Irish, wore their hair Irish. The Burkes of Connacht went the whole distance and simply became a Gaelic lordship, with a Gaelic title, MacWilliam Burke, inaugurated in the Gaelic manner. Some of them retained gallowglass. Some of them levied coign and livery on their own tenants. And they fought the crown's other subjects, and sometimes the crown, exactly as an Irish lord would.

*[<< The Anglo-Irish poets point deserves a sentence of its own: bardic poetry in classical Irish was composed for Fitzgerald and Burke patrons in the same metres and to the same conventions as for O'Neill and O'Brien. The high culture of Gaelic Ireland was, by 1450, being funded by the descendants of the invaders. >>]*

Against this, in 1366, a parliament at Kilkenny passed the statutes that bear its name, and they are the centrepiece of the period.

The **Statutes of Kilkenny** forbid, in thirty-five articles and in a Norman French of magnificent exasperation, essentially everything described in the paragraph above. No marriage with the Irish. No fosterage, no gossipred, no concubinage with them. No use of the Irish language among the English, on pain of forfeiture of lands. No Irish name, no Irish dress, no riding in the Irish fashion without a saddle. No brehon law — its use by an Englishman is declared treason. No Irish poets or minstrels among the English, because they spy. No Irish religious house may admit an Englishman, and no English house an Irishman. No selling of horses or armour to the Irish in time of war. No coign and livery, ever, by anyone.

Read as a law, it is a bold assertion of separation, an iron line drawn between the two nations, the colonial state pulling itself together. Read as evidence, which is how it should be read, it is a **confession**, and a total one. A parliament does not prohibit what nobody is doing. Every article of the Statutes of Kilkenny is a photograph of a practice so widespread that the government of the colony felt obliged to make it a felony. The Statutes tell us that the English of Ireland were, in 1366, speaking Irish, marrying Irish, fostering Irish, versifying in Irish, litigating in Irish law, and billeting troops on the country in the Irish manner, in numbers large enough to alarm the Dublin parliament into a comprehensive legislative panic.

And there is a second irony, which the reader may be left to find. The men who passed the Statutes of Kilkenny were themselves the English of Ireland. Many of them spoke Irish. Some of them were married to Irish wives. The parliament was presided over by Lionel of Clarence, a son of the king of England, sent over with an army and a mandate to fix the colony, who went home in short order having fixed nothing; but the men in the chamber were colonists, legislating against their own lives, and the law they passed was, in the event, enforced patchily where the crown was strong and not at all where it was weak — which is to say, precisely where it was needed.

The Statutes were reissued and re-enacted, in whole or in part, for two hundred years. That is the measure of their success.

### The church between two nations

The church divided along the same seam as everything else, and it divided early. By the fourteenth century contemporaries wrote quite naturally of an *ecclesia inter Anglicos* and an *ecclesia inter Hibernicos* — a church among the English and a church among the Irish — and meant it institutionally: dioceses in the colony and dioceses in the Gaelic lordships, staffed from their own populations, with their own languages and their own patrons and, increasingly, their own quarrels. Religious houses legislated for the exclusion of the other nation exactly as the Statutes of Kilkenny did, and had been doing so before the Statutes said they must. The most interesting movement of the period runs the other way: the observant reform of the fifteenth century, a wave of austere renewal among the friars, which took root most vigorously in **Gaelic** Ireland and in the gaelicised lordships of the west, and which put a network of reformed Franciscan houses across precisely the parts of the island the Dublin government had lost. It is a fact of some consequence for the next century, because when the Reformation comes, it will find in the west a Catholicism that has just been renewed from within, is popular, is preached in Irish, and is not in the least decayed or waiting to be rescued.

### Three nations, and the word for it

By 1450 the phrase *two nations* no longer described the island, and the vocabulary of the period is the best record we have of the ground moving.

The Dublin administration begins to distinguish the *English by blood* from the *English by birth* — that is, the colonists from the men sent over from England — and it makes that distinction precisely because the two have come apart and can no longer be assumed to have the same interests. The Gaelic sources, which had always called the newcomers *Gaill*, foreigners, begin to speak of *Sean-Ghaill*, the old foreigners, as against newer arrivals. Everyone, on both sides of the line, is reaching for new words, and they are reaching for them because the old ones have stopped working.

What the words are groping toward already had a name, and the name was an insult, and it was coined in 1317. The Remonstrance, in the middle of its indictment, pauses to make a distinction that the Dublin administration would not manage for another century: it separates the English of England from the colonists in Ireland, and it calls the latter <cite index="22-1">a *middle nation* — so unlike the English of England, and unlike any other nation, that they are best described as a nation not of middling but of extreme perfidy.</cite>

The pun is doing real work. *Media natio*: a nation in the middle, between two others, belonging fully to neither — and therefore, the Remonstrance says, not middling at all in the one quality that matters, which is treachery. It is a hostile description and it is a wholly accurate one. Six hundred years of Irish history are contained in the observation that by 1317 the descendants of the invaders were already a third thing, and that the Gaelic Irish could see it before the English could.

So: a middle nation. A people who are English to the Gael and Irish to the English, who hold their land by English title and their loyalties by Irish custom, who are the king's subjects in law and nobody's subjects in fact. They will not have a name of their *own* until the sixteenth century, when a new wave of arrivals forces one on them — and the name they get, *Old English*, will be a demotion dressed as a description. That story belongs to Chapter 3. For the fifteenth century the point is simply that the middle nation exists, that it governs most of Ireland, and that the crown does not know what to do about it.

---

## 5. The Pale, and the Ascendancy of the Earls

The Pale is the image everyone retains from this period, and it is worth getting right, because the standard picture — the shrinking beachhead, English Ireland reduced to four counties, the light going out — is true as a description of *royal government* and quite false as a description of what was actually happening.

The word is late and the thing is real. By the fifteenth century the area of effective royal authority had contracted to a coastal strip around Dublin, running roughly from Dundalk to Dalkey and inland perhaps thirty miles at its widest: Dublin, Meath, Louth, Kildare. And in the 1490s the Dublin parliament ordered it physically **fenced** — a double ditch, six feet high, with a thorn hedge, along the landward frontier, to stop the raiders and the cattle. Ireland's medieval history contains few objects more eloquent than that ditch: the king of England's Irish government, four hundred years after the conquest, digging a trench around itself.

But now look outside the ditch, and the picture changes entirely. Beyond the Pale, English *government* had failed. English **power** had not. The Fitzgeralds of Kildare, the Butlers of Ormond, the Fitzgeralds of Desmond — these were the descendants of the invaders, holding vast territories, commanding armies, and by any ordinary measure they were the most powerful men in Ireland. What had happened was not that they had been defeated. It was that they had **stopped being the crown's**, in any sense the crown could rely on, while remaining the crown's in law.

And in 1470 the crown drew the only conclusion available to it: if you cannot govern Ireland against the earls of Kildare, govern it through them. For most of the period from 1470 to 1534, the Fitzgerald earls of Kildare were the king's deputies in Ireland, and they governed it — capably, cheaply, and on their own terms. Garret Mór FitzGerald, the Great Earl, ruled Ireland for the better part of forty years, through Yorkist kings and Tudor ones, by exactly the methods a Gaelic overlord would have recognised: a web of marriage alliances reaching deep into the Gaelic lordships, a private army, hostages, and a reputation for winning. He was fluent in Irish. He married his daughters to O'Neill and O'Connor and Burke. He kept gallowglass. He crowned a pretender, Lambert Simnel, king of England in Christ Church cathedral in 1487, and was forgiven, because Henry VII had no better option; the king is supposed to have said that if all Ireland could not rule this man, then this man should rule all Ireland, and whether or not he said it, it was the policy.

This is the fifteenth-century settlement, and it should be stated without sentiment: the English lordship of Ireland was, for two generations, governed successfully by an Irish-speaking magnate with a private army, as a family enterprise, at almost no cost to the English exchequer. It was cheap. It kept the peace tolerably. Its price was that the crown's authority in Ireland had become a Fitzgerald possession.

There was one attempt to break out of this, and its failure is instructive. In 1494 Henry VII sent over Sir Edward Poynings with an army and a brief to bring Ireland to heel, and Poynings' parliament at Drogheda passed the law that carries his name: no Irish parliament could meet, and no bill could be introduced in it, without the prior approval of the king and council in England. **Poynings' Law** subordinated the Irish legislature to the English executive, and it would remain on the books, a standing grievance, until 1782. It is, on paper, the assertion of English control over Ireland.

On the ground it changed nothing, because the crown could not pay for what it had asserted. Poynings' expedition cost money England did not want to spend. Within two years Henry VII had restored Kildare to the deputyship and gone back to governing Ireland the cheap way. The most famous constitutional instrument of the medieval lordship was passed by a government that then immediately conceded that it could not enforce the policy the instrument was designed to serve.

And one more thing must be said, because the vocabulary of decline conceals it. This was not a dark age. The fifteenth century was, in the Gaelic lordships, a period of high cultural confidence: the bardic schools were flourishing, the professional learned families — Ó Dálaigh, Mac an Bhaird, Ó hUiginn — were at the height of their prestige, the great manuscript compilations were being made, classical Irish poetry was reaching a technical perfection it would never surpass. Medicine, law, and history were being practised by hereditary learned kindreds to a standard that bore comparison with anywhere in Europe. Gaelic lords built tower houses and endowed friaries. And the gaelicised Anglo-Irish magnates paid for a good deal of it. What was declining, in fifteenth-century Ireland, was the English administration in Dublin. Almost nothing else was.

In 1500, a well-informed observer in London might reasonably have concluded that the English interest in Ireland was finished — that the colony had failed, that the settlers had gone native, that the country was governed by a magnate the crown could not control, and that the whole three-hundred-year enterprise had come to nothing. It would have been a fair reading of the evidence. It would have been entirely wrong, and it would have been wrong for reasons no one in 1500 could have seen, because they had not happened yet and did not have to.

## 6. The Ground Shifts

In 1534 the ninth earl of Kildare was summoned to London and detained. His son, Thomas FitzGerald — Silken Thomas, the chroniclers call him — rode to the council chamber at St Mary's Abbey in Dublin, flung down the sword of state, and renounced his allegiance to Henry VIII.

It was a serious rebellion, and it was crushed, and the crushing was of a different order from anything the fifteenth century had seen. Henry sent an army with artillery. Artillery ended the tower house as a serious military proposition and it ended the Fitzgerald ascendancy: Maynooth was battered into surrender and its garrison executed, the earl's kin were hunted down, and in 1537 Silken Thomas and five of his uncles were hanged, drawn, and quartered at Tyburn. The house of Kildare, which had governed Ireland for sixty years, was destroyed in three.

And the crown drew a conclusion. Indirect rule through a great magnate had failed — had produced, in fact, a magnate powerful enough to rebel. Ireland would henceforth be governed from Dublin by English officials, with an English army, at English expense. That decision, taken in the 1530s for immediate and practical reasons, is the beginning of everything that happens in the next chapter.

Two other things had changed, and they had changed underneath, while nobody in Ireland was looking.

The first is that Henry VIII had broken with Rome. Ireland was now, from London's point of view, a Catholic country attached to a Protestant crown, and a Catholic country lying open on the western flank of a realm that suddenly had continental enemies — France, Spain, the Empire, and a pope with an interest in seeing Henry deposed. Ireland stopped being a nuisance and a drain, and became a **strategic liability**. Ground that is a liability gets garrisoned, and garrisoned ground gets settled.

The second is that the crown changed the theory. In 1541 an Irish parliament — attended, notably, by Gaelic lords as well as colonists — passed an act declaring Henry VIII **King of Ireland**. Not lord. King. The distinction is not cosmetic. The lordship had been held from the pope, by a papal grant that the Remonstrance had spent 1317 trying to have revoked, and a pope who had now excommunicated Henry could plausibly revoke it in earnest. The kingdom was held from nobody. And a kingdom, unlike a lordship, contains **subjects** — all of them, everywhere on the island, Gael and colonist alike, equally and without distinction.

The policy that followed was called **surrender and regrant**, and on its own terms it was elegant, generous, and rational. A Gaelic lord would surrender his lands to the king and receive them back immediately by royal charter, holding them henceforth by English law and English title, with an English peerage attached: O'Neill becomes earl of Tyrone, O'Brien becomes earl of Thomond, Burke becomes earl of Clanricarde. Brehon law, tanistry, coign and livery, the whole apparatus of the war-band lordship, would be given up. In exchange, the lord got security of title, a seat in parliament, and the protection of the crown he had just joined. Everyone becomes a subject. Everyone is under one law. The two nations dissolve, by act of parliament, into one.

It is the most attractive settlement anyone offered Ireland in four hundred years, and it might conceivably have worked, and there is no reason in the documents of 1541 to think it could not. It failed for reasons that were not yet visible in 1541: because a Gaelic lord's title had never been his to surrender, being held of his kindred and not of himself, so that the charter that made him secure in English law made him a usurper in Irish; because the crown's men on the ground had appetites the policy did not satisfy; and above all because within a generation the crown would be Protestant in earnest, and the question of who was a subject would be answered not by the surrender of a title but by the taking of an oath.

The parliament of 1541 dissolved the two nations on paper. What actually happened is the next chapter, and it is the reason there is one.

**Robin Frame, *Colonial Ireland 1169–1369* (1981; 2nd edn 2012).**
The spine of this chapter: the lordship as a functioning provincial society rather than a doomed occupation, argued in under 150 pages. Revisionist in temperament — the title says "colonial" and the book then spends itself making the word behave, resisting both the nationalist reading of an alien tyranny and the imperial one of a civilising mission. It stops in 1369 and therefore leaves the century of deepest gaelicisation to others.

**Seán Duffy, *Ireland in the Middle Ages* (1997).**
The best short synthesis for a reader coming to the period cold, and the corrective to any account in which the Gaelic Irish merely react to what is done to them. Duffy is the authority on the Bruce invasion, and this chapter's §3 leans on him. Compact enough to be read in an evening and generous enough to be worth a second one.

**Katharine Simms, *From Kings to Warlords: The Changing Political Structure of Gaelic Ireland in the Later Middle Ages* (1987).**
The indispensable book, and the source of this chapter's central claim about the Gaelic recovery: what expanded after 1350 was not a survival of the old order but a militarised adaptation of it — gallowglass, coign and livery, the sacral king becoming a warlord. It demolishes the romantic picture of an unchanging Gaeldom patiently outlasting the invader, and it demolishes it from inside the Irish sources. Demanding, but the argument is worth the work.

**Art Cosgrove (ed.), *A New History of Ireland, Vol. II: Medieval Ireland 1169–1534* (1987).**
The reference standard, to be consulted rather than read: the place where contested details are adjudicated and where the reader who wants the evidence behind any assertion here will find it. Institutionally revisionist by construction — a committee of specialists rarely produces a martyrology. Expensive, and in a good library.

**Maria Kelly, *A History of the Black Death in Ireland* (2001).**
The specialist study behind §3, and the place to go for the evidence beneath the estimates used here. Its virtue is candour about how little can be known: the mortality figures for medieval Ireland are inferences from chroniclers and abandoned holdings, not counts, and Kelly says so. Establishes the differential impact on the colony as a demographic fact with political consequences, which is this chapter's use of it.

**J. F. Lydon, "The Middle Nation," in *The English in Medieval Ireland* (1984).**
Short, and the origin of the framing that §4 turns on: the colonists as a third thing, neither English nor Irish, and aware of it. Lydon takes the phrase from the Remonstrance, where it is an accusation, and turns it into an analytic category without softening it. If one essay is to be read on the identity of the medieval settler population, this is it.

**Steven Ellis, *Ireland in the Age of the Tudors 1447–1603* (1998).**
Carries §§5–6, and is the sharpest advocate of reading Ireland as an English *borderland* rather than a colony: the Pale as an analogue of the Anglo-Scottish march, the Kildare ascendancy as normal magnate government in a normal periphery. He is one pole of the argument this chapter stages and should be read as an advocate, not as neutral background. His deliberate flattening of the ethnic difference between Gael and colonist is his method's greatest strength and its most contested move.

**Nicholas Canny, *Making Ireland British 1580–1650* (2001).**
Cited here only as the other pole, and pointing forward: Canny reads the Tudor and Stuart project as colonialism in the full sense, with an ideology of dispossession and a settler mentality continuous with the Atlantic empire. Ellis versus Canny is the live disagreement in the field and the reader should meet both. The book itself belongs to Chapter 3 and is treated properly there.

**Gerald of Wales, *The History and Topography of Ireland* and *The Conquest of Ireland* (1180s; trans. J. J. O'Meara, A. B. Scott and F. X. Martin).**
The propaganda of the conquest, written by a Norman-Welsh cleric with a family stake in it, and worthless as evidence about the Irish. Priceless as evidence about the conquerors: it is the fountainhead of the caricature that runs unbroken to the Victorian cartoons, and reading it beside *Punch* in the 1860s is an education in the durability of a libel. Cite it *as* the disease, never as the diagnosis.

**The Statutes of Kilkenny (1366)** and **the Remonstrance of the Irish Princes (1317)**.
The two indispensable documents, and both are short enough to read whole. The Statutes are a photograph of the gaelicisation they prohibit; the Remonstrance is the case against English rule, fully formed, six hundred years before the Proclamation. Both are in print in accessible editions and neither should be met only through a historian's paraphrase.
