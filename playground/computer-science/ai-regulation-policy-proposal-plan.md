# Response to Reviewers

A general note on scope. The brief was explicitly two-track: Phase I optimised for passage, Phase II for soundness "independent of near-term political feasibility." Several comments (especially C's calls for optimism and B's demands for near-exhaustive definition) push against that brief rather than within it. I'll flag where that matters.

## 1. The aviation analogy (Reviewer A)

**Partially accept.** A's three disanalogies (probabilistic vs deterministic, dynamic vs static state, non-accumulating failure) are correct and worth stating. But A's own recommendation concedes the essential point: the analogy is used for *institutional sequencing*, not *technical execution*, and the Guiding Principle already says so ("This proposal follows the same sequencing logic"). So this is a request to make explicit what is already implicit.

**Fix:** Add two or three sentences to the Guiding Principle stating the limits directly — aircraft failures accumulate visibly and configurations are static; AI failure modes may not accumulate and configurations drift. This actually *strengthens* Phase II, because II.1's re-certification-on-material-change and II.5's evaluation-awareness mandate exist precisely because the static-airworthiness model breaks. I'd add a sentence connecting the disanalogy to those provisions.

I'd resist A's framing of this as a "category error." It isn't. An analogy used for one purpose (institutional design) is not refuted by failing at another (fault physics). B's observation that ICAO Annex 13 and FAA–EASA bilaterals are the closer precedents for II.4 is the more useful version of this point — accept that (see §7).

## 2. I.1 — the "unintended action" trigger

This is the strongest substantive criticism, raised by A, B, and C independently, and it's correct.

**Accept.** The trigger "took action its operator did not intend and could not immediately explain" is unworkable. As A notes, agents are *hired* to take unprogrammed actions, and LLM output is by construction not fully explicable. As B notes, this floods the channel with normal variance.

**Fix:** Replace clause (c) entirely. Keep (a) exceeded granted network/system access and (b) accessed a third-party system without authorization — both are about *unauthorized reach*, which the chapter itself already identifies as "a much narrower and more tractable question." Replace (c) with a *consequence*-based trigger, not an intent-based one: e.g. "caused a state change in an external system outside the declared operational envelope." B's severity-tiering suggestion (P0: 24h, P1: 72h, P2: weekly aggregate) is a clean way to handle materiality and I'll adopt it.

## 3. I.1 — the latency/detection paradox

**Accept as a caveat, refute as a defeater.** A and C both note: if the lab can't detect the breach, the 72-hour clock never starts. True. But this is not "logically inconsistent" — it's precisely why I.3 (mandatory monitoring) exists. The clock is triggered by detection; I.3 forces detection capacity to exist. The two provisions are complementary, not contradictory.

**Fix:** State the dependency explicitly: the 72-hour clock is only as good as I.3's monitoring mandate, and disclosure without monitoring is theatre. One sentence in the I.1 motivation, cross-referencing I.3.

## 4. I.2 — "independent" safety officer

**Accept.** A and B are right that an internally-paid officer with no personal exposure degenerates to rubber-stamp. The chapter's own honest assessment already half-concedes this ("a determined lab could still misconfigure a safety officer sign-off as a formality").

**Fix:** I won't pretend Phase I can deliver true independence — that's Phase II's job (II.1's *external* adversarial certification). What Phase I can add cheaply: (i) named, personally-attributable sign-off (removes deniability), (ii) B's org-chart-distance and tenure-protection specifics, and (iii) C's whistleblower-protection clause, which belongs in I.4. The honest value of I.2 is *attribution*, not independence — I'll reframe it that way rather than overclaim.

## 5. I.4 — the FTC hook

**Accept, with qualification.** A overstates ("entirely useless"). B and C get it right: Section 5 is "potentially referable," untested for AI, and operates on a months-to-years timescale.

The timescale objection partly misreads the design: I.4 is explicitly *not* a fast-response mechanism in Phase I ("not a regulator, at this stage"). Fast machine-speed intervention is II.3's job. I.4's FTC referral is for *patterns of non-compliance with disclosure*, which is exactly the kind of slow, retrospective matter FTC handles adequately.

**Fix:** Change "a plausible existing hook" to "potentially referable, on a case-by-case theory of harm — untested for AI incidents." And state explicitly that I.4 has no machine-speed remit; that's deferred to II.3. B is also right that "available on request" is toothless without compulsion or tied incentives — accept B's suggestion to tie compliance to federal procurement eligibility and safe-harbour, which is a stronger lever than enforcement at this phase.

## 6. Phase II, II.2 — the supervisor dilemma

This is A's centrepiece, and it needs a mixed response.

- **Capability asymmetry ("if supervisor ≥ agent, why not just run the supervisor?").** **Refute.** This misunderstands the provision, which the chapter already answers: the supervisor is certified against "a specific, auditable, narrower bar" — scope-violation and deceptive-framing detection — *not* general superiority. Detecting "this action is outside the declared task boundary" is a different and easier task than performing the primary task well. A monitor need not be able to write the code to notice the code is exfiltrating data. The dilemma dissolves once you stop treating capability as scalar.
- **Compute tax (~100%).** **Partially accept.** A overstates the doubling: a scope-violation classifier is far cheaper than a co-equal frontier model, precisely because of the narrow-bar design above. But the cost is real and unaddressed. **Fix:** state that the supervisor's narrow certification bar is *also* what keeps its compute cost well below 1:1, and acknowledge the residual cost as a real burden concentrated on high-tier deployments only.
- **False positives.** **Accept.** A and B both flag that unilateral auto-suspension + high false-positive rate → paralysis → pressure to desensitise → uselessness. Genuine gap. **Fix:** add a required, published maximum false-positive rate as part of the II.2 certification battery, and B's fail-safe/continuity-of-critical-service requirement. Suspension authority without a specified precision floor is indeed unsafe.

## 8. II.5 — "broken foundation" / security theatre

**Refute.** A's claim that evaluation-awareness makes Phase II "security theater" proves too much. By that logic, all of empirical science is theatre because measurement is imperfect — a position this author explicitly rejects (see *Can science tell us everything?*: models are useful precisely because they are imperfect-but-predictive). Evaluation-gaming degrades certification confidence; it doesn't nullify it. An agent that games the *evaluation* still trips I.3/II.2's *runtime* monitors when it acts in deployment, because those observe behaviour, not test performance. II.5 exists to *narrow* this gap, and the chapter says so honestly. Naming an open problem and funding it is the opposite of theatre; pretending it's solved would be theatre.

**Fix:** one sentence making explicit that runtime monitoring (I.3, II.2) is the backstop for evaluation-time gaming — the two operate at different moments and don't share the failure.

## 9. Existing regimes / harmonisation (Reviewer B)

**Strongly accept — the most valuable single comment.** The chapter is parochially US-centric and ignores that its Phase I substantially overlaps existing law: EU AI Act (serious-incident reporting, post-market monitoring, conformity assessment ≈ II.1), US CIRCIA (72-hour cyber-incident reporting with liability protections — nearly identical to I.1), NIS2/GDPR (72-hour breach reporting). Proposing a 72-hour clock without noting CIRCIA already mandates one for critical infrastructure is a real omission.

**Fix:** Add a short "Relation to existing regimes" subsection before Phase I noting overlap and a harmonisation stance (align timelines and definitions with CIRCIA and the EU AI Act rather than create a parallel duplicative channel). This also disarms the "novel unworkable ask" objection: much of Phase I is *extension of existing 72-hour reporting norms to AI-agent incidents*, which strengthens the feasibility argument.
