Canon — From St. Patrick to the Celtic Tiger: A History of Ireland#

Authoritative spine for the project. Every chapter chat fetches this first (via init.md). When a decision changes, change it here — this file wins over any chat’s memory. Draft 0.1 of the canon; edit freely.

Subject#

The book’s subject is Ireland and its diaspora — not the island alone, and not the Republic alone. This decision keeps Ulster Protestants inside the story as full participants rather than as an awkward exception, and it keeps the global Irish (the emigrant nation many times larger than the island’s population) inside the frame as an actor that funds the Land League, arms and bankrolls the revolution, and helps broker the peace.

Title#

Working title: From St. Patrick to the Celtic Tiger: A History of Ireland. Kept as a deliberate, knowingly-inaccurate mismatch — the two poles are wildly disparate and neither is a literal endpoint; the reader will forgive the gesture. Placeholder; may change.

Scope and endpoint#

Two millennia, from the island before England (c. 1 CE) to the island after the Good Friday Agreement. The account closes in spring 2026 and does not pretend to be the last word on a history still in motion.

Structure — 11 chapters (9 chronological + 2 crosscutting)#

Broad chronological order, with the five paradoxes as connecting threads. Dates below are the working boundaries from the agreed periodization; titles for Ch 1–2 are unsettled (two proposals each — decide).

Title

Dates

Notes

1

Gaelic Ireland (alt: The World Before England)

1 CE – 1169

The society Rome never conquered; kin-based order; coming of Christianity; monastic golden age and its missionaries; Vikings and their towns; eve of the Norman arrival.

2

The Lordship and the Two Nations (alt: Conquest and Its Limits)

1169 – 1541

Norman invasion; the lordship; the long stalemate — settlers gaelicise, English grip contracts to the Pale; neither free nor subdued.

3

Conquest and Plantation

1541 – 1691

Tudor reconquest; the Reformation arriving as English imposition (religion and nationality fuse); Munster and Ulster plantations; 1641; Cromwell; the Williamite war to Limerick. Fault lines cast in iron.

4

The Protestant Ascendancy

1691 – 1798

Penal Laws; Grattan’s Parliament; the Anglo-Irish world; Enlightenment; the United Irishmen; ending in the 1798 rebellion.

5

Union and Catholic Nation

1798 – 1845

Act of Union; O’Connell; Catholic Emancipation; the invention of mass constitutional politics; the pre-Famine demographic pressure cooker.

6

The Great Hunger and After

1845 – 1891

The Famine; mass death and emigration; the demographic rupture; Fenianism; the Land War; Parnell and the fusion of land and Home Rule. The book’s hinge.

7

Revolution and Partition

1891 – 1923

Cultural revival; Ulster crisis; third Home Rule bill; 1916; War of Independence; Treaty; partition; Civil War. Densest and most contested chapter.

8

Two Irelands

1923 – 1998

The Free State/Republic’s path and Northern Ireland’s path running in parallel — Stormont, discrimination, civil rights, the Troubles — converging on the Good Friday Agreement.

9

1998 to Today

1998 – 2026

The four inversions (see below). Standalone chapter.

10

Religion in Ireland (crosscutting)

—

Christianity without Roman conquest; the distinctive monastic church; the failure of the Reformation in Ireland; the post-Famine devotional revolution; the astonishingly rapid collapse since the 1990s. Lived religion over official narrative.

11

Near-Shore Colony / The Diaspora (crosscutting)

—

Was Ireland a colony, a kingdom, or a constitutional anomaly? Ireland set against India, Scotland, Wales, the white-settler dominions. The global Irish — Boston, New York, Liverpool, Australia, Argentina — and their feedback into the homeland’s politics.

The four inversions (Ch 9 organising thesis): (1) demographic — the engineered Protestant majority of 1921 gone, Catholic-background plurality in the 2021 census, first nationalist First Minister 2024; (2) political — the physical-force tradition domesticated, Sinn Féin administering the state it once fought; (3) religious — one of Europe’s most devout societies becoming one of its most liberal (marriage equality 2015, abortion repeal 2018); (4) economic — the perennial exporter of people becoming a country of net immigration. Connective tissue: peace process consolidating and stalling, Brexit reopening the border question, the live border-poll question as the open ending.

The Five Paradoxes (intro spine)#

Each is a pattern that seems to contradict itself, not a riddle with a tidy answer. Diaspora and literary excellence are woven through rather than standing as their own paradoxes.

  1. A nation that kept its identity but lost its tongue — kept one of Europe’s most tenacious identities while very nearly losing the language that carried it. Catholicism becomes the carrier that language ceased to be.

  2. The near-shore colony — a day’s sail from an expansionist power; conquered, settled, governed for centuries, never absorbed. Colony or constitutional anomaly? Kept open.

  3. The returning light — converted late and from the edge, Ireland re-evangelised the continental centre. First instance of the margin marks the centre — recurs in the writers (four Nobel laureates) and emigrants.

  4. The demographic wound — still below the 1845 population; catastrophe followed not by recovery but by a century of draining away; the diaspora (70–80 million claiming descent) woven in here, and the subject-choice (“Ireland and its diaspora”) stated.

  5. The long war that ended — a four-centuries-old confessional-and-colonial quarrel surviving into the age of the motorway, then largely stopping with surprising speed. Double puzzle: why it lasted, how it ended. Sets up the closing chapters.

Historiographical stance#

Navigate between two poles, holding both with equal seriousness without false symmetry:

  • Pole A — the English view of the Irish: at mildest patronising (Paddy and his pig), at worst the Victorian simian caricature; suffering earned, character the explanation. The literature of the stronger party.

  • Pole B — the glamourised Irish: noble heroes in an unbroken struggle, martyrs spotless, statues and street names, silence on the crimes done in the cause and the internecine bloodletting. The literature of the wounded party.

The book holds with the revisionist position (the Irish Historical Studies tradition, Roy Foster most prominently) as the balanced achievement — but not as a bland midpoint, because the asymmetries of power are real (England conquered Ireland, not the reverse; Irish peasants starved; the Catholic majority was excluded from power in its own country). Post-revisionist caution registered too: there is such a thing as a true grievance. The Famine-genocide question is the live wire — presented in full, not resolved by slogan. Numbers in dispute (Famine dead, pre-Norman population, 1798 toll, Troubles toll) are given as ranges; uncertainty is named.

Analytical commitments (gathered from across the project — confirm these still hold)#

  • Against determinism — earlier actors must not appear to march toward an inevitable outcome; preserve open contingency (the Schwabe “backward-reading dramatic irony” admired, but must not slide into teleology).

  • Messy reality over clean narrative — surface complexity, contradiction, friction over tidy explanatory arcs.

  • Survivorship bias and motivated reasoning as explicit lenses — foreground what gets forgotten or suppressed, and why.

  • Separate distinct fractures — e.g. in Ch 7, partition and the Treaty split are two ruptures, not one.

  • The revival as “capture” — the cultural revival’s radicalisation framed as institutional/ideological capture, not as an expression of inherent separatism.

  • Lived religion over official narrative — syncretism and hybrid belief over top-down doctrine (Ch 10 especially).

  • Epistemic honesty — careful distinctions, acknowledged uncertainty, engagement with genuine historiographical debate, including where consensus is unsettled.

Conventions#

  • Length: ~5,000 words per chapter, order of magnitude, deliberately weighted toward the modern period where sources and drama concentrate.

  • Numbers in dispute: give ranges, name the uncertainty.

  • Companion volume (the Jewish book): a light echo, never a scaffold. No explicit reference to it in the body. The Irish–Jewish comparison lives in one short section (“A Word on Another People”): small peoples marking the world beyond their numbers, diasporas larger than the homeland born of catastrophe, histories thick with atrocity — sharpened by the language contrast (Jews kept texts and sacred language; Irish nearly lost the language and kept identity anyway). Do not press the parallel.

  • Apparatus: Glossary and Index of Names (persons/peoples/dynasties, places, institutions/organisations/events). Build the glossary early.

  • Cross-references: chapter links as ireland-N.md; [x] markers are placeholders pending the final map.

  • Closing matter (intro): a list of principal books cited, each with a ~5-line note on its main contribution and its stance in the revisionism debate.

Glossary seed (expand into glossary.md)#

  • Gael — a member of the Gaelic, Irish-speaking population; the native Irish.

  • Gall (pl. Gaill) — “foreigner”; applied first to Vikings, then to Normans and English.

  • túath — the basic petty-kingdom, the political unit of early Gaelic Ireland.

  • brehon — a jurist of early Irish customary (“Brehon”) law.

  • Old English — descendants of the medieval Anglo-Norman settlers, largely Catholic, distinct by the 16th–17th c. from newer arrivals.

  • New English — Protestant settlers and officials arriving from the Tudor period onward.

  • Plantation — confiscation of land and settlement of (mostly Protestant, British) colonists, esp. Munster and Ulster, 16th–17th c.

  • (Protestant) Ascendancy — the Anglican landowning elite dominant in the 18th c.

  • Penal Laws — laws restricting the rights of Catholics (and Dissenters) from the late 17th c.

  • Home Rule — the movement (later 19th–early 20th c.) for a devolved Irish parliament within the UK.

  • Fenian — member/supporter of the IRB tradition (from 1858); broadly, a militant separatist.

  • Fianna Fáil / Fine Gael — the two parties emerging from the pro-/anti-Treaty split of the Civil War.

  • Dáil (Éireann) — the parliament (lower house) of independent Ireland; first convened 1919.

  • Oireachtas — the parliament of Ireland (Dáil, Seanad, President).

  • Taoiseach — head of government of the Republic.

  • (Recurring question to keep live: who is “Irish” at any given date — the Gaelic population, the gaelicised Normans, the Ascendancy, the whole island, or the nation claimed by the national movement.)

Workflow (reference)#

Three modes, one source of truth (the repo):

  • Chapter chats — drafting and argument, one per chapter; each starts with init.md (fetch canon + style + chapter).

  • Cowork (folder: ~/PycharmProjects/essays/bagatelles) — cross-file work: consistency checks, timeline assembly, reference gathering, applying style.md across chapters.

  • Claude Code — the scripted end: multi-model (Mistral/Gemini/GPT) review with a designated editor model; human has the last word.

Rule: commit, then init — init.md fetches the last pushed version, so push before starting a session.
