From St. Patrick to the Celtic Tiger:
A History of Ireland#

Johannes Siedersleben (Editor)

June 2026

Five Paradoxes#

Irish history poses questions that do not arise, or do not arise the same way, for other nations. None of them is a riddle with a tidy answer; each is a paradox — a pattern that seems to contradict itself, and that the rest of the book exists to make sense of. Here are five.

1. A nation that kept its identity but lost its tongue#

Most peoples who survive as a distinct nation do so carried by a language. The language is the vessel: it holds the literature, the law, the prayers, the private jokes, the boundary between us and them. Lose the language and the identity usually dissolves within a generation or two into the surrounding majority.

Ireland did the opposite. It kept one of the most tenacious national identities in Europe while very nearly losing the language that had carried it for two thousand years. In 1800 a majority of the population on the island still spoke Irish; within a century it had collapsed to a remnant on the western seaboard, hollowed out by the Famine, by emigration, by an education system that punished the language, and by the cold logic that English was the tongue of advancement. And yet Irishness did not dissolve. The sense of being a people apart — with its own grievances, memory, music, and claim to the land — survived the loss of the very thing that is supposed to make such survival possible.

The paradox is how. Part of the answer is religion: Catholicism became the carrier of identity that language had ceased to be, the marker that separated the Irish from the Protestant colonial order. Part is grievance and memory, transmitted in English itself. And part is that the loss was never total — a revival movement in the late nineteenth century made the dying language into a national symbol precisely as it ceased to be a daily reality (chapters [x] and [x]). But the puzzle stands: a nation that proves a people can outlive its own language.

2. The near-shore colony#

Around the year 1, the Roman Empire stood on the British shore, looked across at Ireland, and declined to cross. That refusal, as the first chapter argues, set up the puzzle that runs through the whole of Irish history.

Ireland sits a day’s sail from one of the most centralising, expansionist powers in human history — and was conquered by it, settled by it, governed by it for centuries, yet never fully absorbed by it. It is the great anomaly of the British Isles: near enough to be permanently within England’s grasp, never near enough — or never quite governable enough — to become simply a part of England the way Cornwall or Wales or the Scottish Lowlands did. For three quarters of a millennium after the Normans landed in 1169, Ireland was something for which the political vocabulary has no clean word: not an independent country, not a settled province, not quite a colony in the overseas sense and yet treated like one, plantation and all. The English kept conquering it because it kept not staying conquered. << mention John Scotus, one of the first and most important medieval philosophers, just as a hook of a short paragraph on him in Chapter 1 >>

The paradox is what Ireland was during those centuries, and the question turns out to be politically loaded to this day. Was it England’s first colony — the laboratory where techniques of plantation, dispossession, and racial contempt were rehearsed before being exported to America and beyond? Or was it a constitutional anomaly within a multi-kingdom archipelago, mis-described by a colonial vocabulary borrowed from elsewhere? How one answers shapes how one reads everything from the plantations to the Famine to the Troubles. This book keeps the question open and keeps asking it.

3. The returning light#

Ireland was almost the last place in Western Europe that Christianity reached: a remote island beyond the edge of the Roman world, converted late, sideways, and by others. By every expectation it should have been a quiet terminus, a place where the faith arrived to settle and fall silent.

Instead it became a wellspring. Within two centuries of receiving Christianity, Ireland was exporting it — sending monks back across the sea to re-found monasteries, copy manuscripts, and re-light the disciplined religious life across a continent that had been Christian centuries before Ireland ever was. As the Roman order collapsed and the structures of learning frayed, Irishmen — Columba on Iona, Columbanus at Luxeuil and Bobbio, Gall in Switzerland, Kilian in Franconia, Aidan in Northumbria — carried books and letters and a peculiarly Irish asceticism into Britain, Gaul, Italy, and the German lands. The periphery re-evangelised the centre; the pupil became the teacher.

The paradox is why the edge outlasted the middle, and it recurs. One can overstate it << cite Cahill, How the Irish Saved Civilisation (1995), as the popular high-water mark of the claim >> — the slogan that the Irish “saved civilisation” flattens a story in which Byzantium, the continental monasteries, and the Islamic world preserved at least as much. But the core is real, and it is the first instance of a pattern this book returns to: Ireland’s very marginality — its distance and smallness and exclusion from the centres of power — becoming at certain moments a strange kind of strength. It shows in the monks and missionaries here; it shows again, much later, in the writers who gave the English language four Nobel laureates and a literature wildly out of proportion to the island’s size, and in the emigrants who carried Irish influence across the world. The margin, more than once, marks the centre.

4. The demographic wound#

Almost every country in the world has more people now than it did in 1840. Ireland is one of the very few that does not.

On the eve of the Great Famine the island held about 8.5 million people. Within a decade roughly a million were dead and a million more had emigrated, and the haemorrhage did not stop — emigration became structural, generation after generation, until by the mid-twentieth century the population had fallen to around half its pre-Famine peak. The whole island today, more than 180 years later, has still not regained the population it had in 1845. No European country experienced anything comparable in the modern era: a demographic catastrophe followed not by recovery but by a century of continued draining away, written into the landscape as abandoned villages — and into the wider world as a global Irish diaspora many times larger than the island itself. Perhaps seventy to eighty million people now claim Irish descent, fifteen or twenty times the population of Ireland; and that scattered nation would, in time, fund the Land League, arm and bankroll the revolution, and broker the peace. The wound abroad became a power that reached back home. It is why this book takes as its subject not the island alone but Ireland and its diaspora.

The paradox here is partly causal and partly moral, and it is the most contested in all of Irish history. A potato blight struck the whole of Europe in the 1840s; only in Ireland did it kill on this scale, because only in Ireland was so much of the population so wholly dependent on the one crop, under a landholding and political order imposed from outside. So was the Famine a natural disaster, a catastrophe of misgovernment and ideology, or — as some have argued — something that meets the threshold of genocide? The bare demographic fact is not in dispute. Almost everything about how to name it is. Chapters [x] and [x] take up both the event and the long argument over what to call it.

5. The long war that ended#

Almost everywhere else in Western Europe, the wars of religion and conquest burned out long ago. The confessional quarrels that tore the continent apart in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries had, by the modern era, cooled into history — settled, forgotten, or absorbed into states that no longer killed over them.

Ireland is the great exception. A conflict whose terms were set four centuries ago — religion, land, conquest, and crown — survived into the age of the motorway and the television, erupting in the late twentieth century as bombs in Belfast, Birmingham, and London, and then, with surprising speed, very largely stopping. The quarrel that the Reformation and the plantations had cast in iron proved astonishingly durable, outliving every comparable European feud, only to be brought — within a single generation, and within living memory — to a settlement that has mostly held. Thirty years of the Troubles, and then the Good Friday Agreement of 1998; the guns, for now, largely silent.

The paradox is double: why it lasted so long, and how it ended at all. The long persistence is the accumulated weight of everything in the chapters before — a grievance never resolved because the underlying questions of who owns the land and who governs the island were never settled, only deferred. The ending is harder to explain and easier to take for granted now that it has happened: a convergence of exhaustion, demographic change, European money and membership, patient diplomacy, and a diaspora that for once financed peace rather than war. The argument the guns were fighting over is not concluded — a united Ireland is now openly discussed rather than merely dreamed or dreaded — but the means have changed, and that change is one of the few unambiguous pieces of good news in the whole long story. The last chapters take up how it happened, and how provisional it may be.

Irish and Jews - a Similar Fate?#

The Irish and the Jews have long invited comparison, and the comparison is worth making briefly — provided it is not pressed too hard, for the differences matter as much as the likenesses.

Both are small peoples who have marked the world far out of proportion to their numbers: the Irish in literature, in the reach of their early missionaries, and in a political influence carried across the world by emigration; the Jews in a record of intellectual achievement equally disproportionate to their numbers. Both have diasporas larger than the homeland itself — the global Irish many times the population of the island, the Jewish dispersion older and wider still — and in both cases the diaspora was produced in large part by catastrophe and flight. And both carry long histories thick with atrocity, dispossession, and the memory of being done to by stronger powers.

But the instructive thing is a difference, and it sits at the heart of this book’s first paradox. The Jews survived two thousand years of dispersion by carrying their texts and their sacred language with them; the language and the law were the thread that held the people together. The Irish survived by the opposite route — they very nearly lost their language and kept their identity anyway. Two of the most tenacious peoples in history, arriving at the same destination, durable nationhood, by opposite means. The parallel is real; it should not be allowed to flatten either story into the other.

Revisionists and the Memory of the Nation#

Irish history is a contested field, and the contest tends to organise itself between two extremes.

At one pole stands the English view of the Irish — the view of the centuries of conquest and condescension, and one that persisted well into the twentieth century. At its mildest it was patronising: Paddy and his pig, the feckless, charming, childlike Celt who could not govern himself and was fortunate to be governed. At its worst it was something darker — the Irish as a lesser breed, simian in the Victorian cartoons, their suffering earned and their character the explanation for it. This is the literature of the stronger party, and it is not extinct.

At the other pole stands the glamourised Irish — the nation as a gallery of noble heroes in an unbroken struggle against the oppressor, their cause pure and their martyrs spotless. It is a tradition that gives the leaders of the rebellions their statues, their memorials, and their street names, and that tends to pass over in silence the crimes done in the cause, the internecine bloodletting, and the moments when the oppressed were also oppressors. This is the literature of the wounded party, and it too is alive and well.

There is no shortage of writing that serves one pole or the other more or less unconditionally. Between them stands the position this book holds with, the one that English-language readers know as the revisionism debate. From the mid-twentieth century, professional Irish historians — the tradition associated with the journal Irish Historical Studies and, later and most prominently, with Roy Foster — set out to professionalise and de-mythologise the national story: to test the inherited nationalist narrative against the documents, to complicate its heroes and its martyrologies, and to insist that history is not the same thing as commemoration. Their work reshaped the field. It was also, inevitably, charged with going too far — with a fastidious neutrality that could shade into apology for the colonial order, with explaining away suffering that the people who endured it had felt as real. The counter-argument, made by historians and critics sometimes grouped as post-revisionist, was that revisionism risked replacing one distortion with another, and that there is such a thing as a true grievance. The balanced view is not a precise midpoint between the two poles — the asymmetries are too real for that — but it is recognisably the revisionists’ achievement, and it is where this book stands.

The most exposed nerve in the whole debate is the Great Famine, and the question of whether it constitutes genocide. The nationalist memory long held that Britain, at best, let the Irish die through ideological indifference and, at worst, intended it. Revisionist scholarship complicated the charge — pointing to the genuine ignorance, the laissez-faire dogma applied across the whole United Kingdom, the absence of an exterminatory plan — without ever quite disposing of the moral weight of a million dead under a government that exported food from a starving country. This book treats that argument as a real reckoning that changed what could honestly be said, to be presented in full rather than resolved by slogan.

The aim throughout is to hold both perspectives with equal seriousness — without lapsing into false symmetry. The asymmetries of power were real: it was England that conquered Ireland and not the reverse; it was Irish, not English, peasants who starved in the 1840s; it was the Catholic majority that was for centuries excluded from power in its own country. And yet the morality tale has its own blind spots: native Irish lords who collaborated and dispossessed; sectarian violence that ran in both directions; a nationalist tradition that could be as capable of myth-making and exclusion as the order it opposed. To recite only one side’s failures is to miss the history. Where the sources are uncertain, the uncertainty is named. Where the numbers are disputed — the Famine dead, the population of pre-Norman Ireland, the toll of 1798 or of the Troubles — the ranges are given.

The same even-handedness applies within Ireland. The book treats Catholic and Protestant, nationalist and unionist, Gaelic and Anglo-Irish, the constitutional tradition and the physical-force tradition, with the same respect — because each of them is part of the story of the island, and none of them holds it whole.

Terms#

Irish history carries a great many unfamiliar terms — Gael and Gall, Old English and New English, plantation and ascendancy, Home Rule and Dáil, Fenian and Fianna, túath and brehon, Taoiseach and Oireachtas — together with the perennial question of who exactly is meant by “Irish” at any given date: the Gaelic population, the Norman settlers who became “more Irish than the Irish,” the Protestant Ascendancy, the whole population of the island, or the nation claimed by the national movement. Whoever does not have these terms to hand will quickly lose the thread.

They are all explained in the Glossary. It is worth a look before reading and a return whenever needed. The Index of Names gathers persons, peoples and dynasties, places, and the institutions, organisations, and events.

Structure#

The book runs in broad chronological order, from the island before England to the island after the Good Friday Agreement, with the recurring paradoxes as its connecting threads. Nine chronological chapters carry the narrative; two crosscutting chapters follow.

Chapter 1 — The World Before England (1 CE–1168). Celtic and Christian Ireland: the society Rome never conquered, the kin-based order, the coming of Christianity, the monastic golden age and its missionaries, the Vikings and their towns, and the eve of the Norman arrival.

Chapter 2 — The Lordship and the Two Nations (1169–1541). The Norman invasion, the lordship of Ireland, and the long stalemate in which the conquest neither completed itself nor failed — the settlers gaelicised, the English grip contracted to the Pale, and the island entered the late Middle Ages neither free nor subdued.

The Present Moment#

This book appears at a time when the settlement that ended Ireland’s long war — the Good Friday Agreement of 1998 — is both holding and under strain. Brexit reopened the question the Agreement had carefully set aside, the status of the border across the island, and with it the longer question the border has always stood for. Demographic and political change in Northern Ireland has made a future referendum on Irish unity a real subject of discussion rather than a slogan, even as the institutions of power-sharing have repeatedly stalled. The guns are, for now, largely silent; the argument they were fighting over is not concluded.

A book that treats a history still in motion cannot be the last word. It can only record the state of things up to a certain point and hope that the structures it describes help the reader make sense of what comes next. This volume closes its account in the spring of 2026.
