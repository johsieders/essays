Chapter 1 — The World Before England (1 CE – 1168)#

1. The Inheritance#

Around the year 1, the Roman world had come as far as Ireland’s doorstep and decided not to step through it. Across a narrow sea the legions held Britain; Roman traders knew the Irish coast and Roman geographers could name the island. But no governor was ever sent, no road ever cut, no tax ever levied. To begin here, at the threshold the Empire declined to cross, is to begin at the fact that shapes everything else: Ireland entered the Common Era inside the orbit of the classical world and yet outside its grasp.

The island the Romans looked at was already ancient. For four thousand years before any of this, people had farmed its valleys, grazed cattle on its hills, and buried their dead in great stone chambers aligned to the turning of the sun. At Newgrange, on a bend of the Boyne, a passage tomb raised around 3200 BCE — older than Stonehenge, older than the pyramids of Giza — had been built so precisely that, on the mornings around the winter solstice, a blade of light reaches down its long throat to illuminate the chamber at its heart. Whoever did that commanded a society capable of organisation, astronomy, and ambition on a scale we tend not to credit to the deep past. That world — the world of the megalith builders — is the substrate. We cannot read its language or recover its gods. But it left the land marked, and those marks would matter, because the people who came later built their own meanings on top of them.

The people who concern us arrived, in cultural terms, somewhere in the last few centuries before the Common Era. They were Celtic — not a race, but a language and a style of life and art that had spread across much of Europe from the Alps to Iberia. By the time it reached the Atlantic edge, Celtic culture in Ireland had taken on the particular form archaeologists call by the name of a Swiss site, La Tène: a world of iron, of horse and chariot, of intricate curvilinear ornament curling across sword-scabbard and gold torc, of a warrior aristocracy and the poets and jurists who served it. The language they brought, a form of Celtic we now call Goidelic, is the direct ancestor of Irish — one of the oldest vernacular languages in Europe to have a continuous written and spoken life down to the present day.

How many people lived on the island then we can only guess; there was no census, and no one was counting. The usual estimates run somewhere between a quarter and half a million souls — a thin population scattered across a heavily forested land in small farming kindreds, with nothing remotely like a town anywhere among them. The figure is soft and should be held loosely, but the order of magnitude is the point: this was a rural, dispersed, intimate society, the kind in which a person’s standing was known to everyone who mattered to them.

So Ireland at the year 1 is not a blank or a beginning. It is a Celtic, Iron Age society, speaking an early form of Irish, layered over a far more ancient inheritance it no longer understood but had not erased. It sits at the western limit of a cultural world that stretches back across the whole continent. And it is about to be defined, for the next thousand years and more, less by what reaches it than by one enormous thing that does not.

2. Ireland and Rome — The Dog That Did Not Bark#

The most important fact about Ireland in the first four centuries of the Common Era is something that did not happen. Rome did not conquer it.

This is easy to state and hard to feel the weight of. Across the narrow sea, Britain was a Roman province for nearly four hundred years — longer than the time separating us from the reign of Elizabeth I. Roman Britain had cities, paved roads, a money economy, written law, taxation, villas with underfloor heating, garrison towns, and an administrative grid laid over the landscape like a net. Latin was the language of power. The whole apparatus of the classical state, with its assumption that authority is territorial and bureaucratic and written down, was a lived daily reality a day’s sail from the Irish coast.

None of it crossed the water. The Romans knew Ireland — they called it Hibernia, traded with it, and at least one Roman writer thought it could be taken and held with a single legion. But the cost-benefit calculation never favoured invasion, and so Ireland remained the largest part of the Celtic world never to be absorbed into the Empire. It was near to Rome — near enough to trade hides and wolfhounds and slaves for Roman wine and silver, near enough that Roman coins and pottery turn up in Irish soil — and yet wholly outside it.

This single absence is the deep structure beneath everything that follows in this chapter, and arguably beneath much of the book. Because Rome never came, Ireland entered recorded history with no towns, no coinage, no standing professional administration, no concept of the state as an impersonal territorial machine, and no Latin grid of governance. When literacy did arrive, it came not with the tax-collector and the magistrate but with the monk and the gospel-book — and it grafted itself onto native institutions rather than replacing them. The Irish learned classes were already there to receive it.

It is worth pausing on the question this raises, because it will return in every later chapter under different costume. Ireland sits just off the shoulder of a far larger, far more centralised neighbour, within reach but never quite within grasp. Is it a near-shore world that happens to have escaped absorption, or a near-shore world whose whole later history is the story of that absorption being attempted again and again by other hands? Rome declined to make Ireland a colony. The question of whether Ireland is a colony — and of whom, and since when — is one this book will keep asking. Rome’s refusal is where the question starts, because it set up the thing that England would later find so maddening and so tempting: a sophisticated, ancient, literate society right next door that had never once been governed the way the conquerors understood government.

3. The Social Order#

What, then, was there instead? Not chaos — that was the colonist’s later libel — but an order of a different kind, one that worked by personal bond rather than territorial command.

The basic unit was the túath, a word usually translated “petty kingdom” but better imagined as a people: a few thousand persons bound by a shared sense of descent and ruled by a king. There were perhaps a hundred and fifty of these on the island at any one time. A túath was not a patch of ground with a border so much as a web of people with a head. Its king, the rí, was the smallest of several grades of king, for kingship in Ireland was tiered: a lesser king was himself the client of an over-king, who might in turn submit to a king of one of the great provincial dynasties. The whole society was held together not by a chain of command running down from a capital but by a lattice of these vertical bonds, renegotiated constantly, by marriage, by hostage-taking, by cattle-raid and submission.

The glue of the whole arrangement was clientship. A lord granted cattle to a free client; the client returned food, labour, and military service. Status was reckoned with an almost legal precision and expressed in cattle and in “honour-price,” the compensation owed if one’s honour were violated — a value that rose with rank and governed everything from the penalty for injuring a man to the weight his oath carried in a dispute. This was a face-to-face world in which who you were, and to whom you were bound, was the organising fact of existence.

Standing somewhat apart from the hierarchy of kings and clients were the learned classes, the áes dána, the “people of art,” and they are central to the Irish difference. There were the filid, the poets — but “poet” undersells them. A fili was a repository of genealogy, history, law, and praise, a maker of reputations whose satire a king feared because it could blister his honour and unmake him. There were the brithemain, the brehons, the jurists who carried and applied the immense body of customary law we call Brehon law — a law not of statutes handed down by a state but of inherited principle, memorised, refined, and applied by specialists, concerned above all with status, contract, injury, and the careful tariff of compensation. And there were the druids, the pre-Christian religious specialists, who would fade as Christianity advanced but whose learned function the new monastic scholars would partly inherit and partly displace.

The thing to hold onto is that this learned class was hereditary, professional, prestigious, and pan-Irish. Its members moved between túatha; their learning was the same across an island that was politically a mosaic. They were the carriers of a high culture that transcended the fragmentation of kings. When Christianity arrived with its books and its Latin, it did not meet a society that despised learning and had to be taught to value it. It met a society that already revered the learned word and merely had to be persuaded to revere a new kind of it. That is why the graft took so deeply and so fast — and why this whole social order, with its honour-prices and its hereditary poets and its kings who were nothing like magistrates, has to be understood now, at the start, because the slow killing of it is the long arc of the chapters to come.

4. The Coming of Christianity#

Every Irish schoolchild knows that Saint Patrick brought Christianity to Ireland. It is a good story and almost the wrong shape.

Christianity did not arrive in Ireland as a single event with a single author. It seeped. By the fourth century the faith was widespread in Roman Britain and Gaul, and Ireland was tied to both by the ordinary traffic of trade and the grim traffic of slavery. Christian merchants came and went; Christian Britons were carried off to Irish farms as captives, Patrick himself being only the most famous; and somewhere in the southeast, the part of the island most enmeshed in commerce with the Roman world, small Christian communities formed before any missionary set out to make them.

We have a precise and revealing date for this. In 431, the chronicler Prosper of Aquitaine records that Pope Celestine sent a bishop named Palladius “to the Irish believing in Christ.” The phrasing repays attention: there were Irish believing in Christ already, in 431, in sufficient number to need a bishop sent to serve them. Palladius was dispatched not to convert a pagan island from scratch but to organise an existing flock. The familiar narrative, in which a heroic newcomer brings the light to a people sitting in darkness, is already complicated by the dry testimony of a continental chronicle. The light was already flickering in the southeast, kindled by trade; what Patrick did was carry it into the darkness of the north and the west.

Patrick belongs to the generation after, in the middle decades of the fifth century, and his importance is real but different from the legend’s. We know him, unusually, in his own words — his Confessio and his open letter denouncing the British warlord Coroticus are among the very few personal voices to reach us from fifth-century western Europe. They show not a serene wonder-worker but an anxious, defensive, deeply driven man, conscious that his Latin was rustic and his learning thin, stung by critics in the British church who questioned his fitness, and possessed by an absolute conviction that God had called him personally back to the island of his captivity. His contribution was to push Christianity beyond the trading communities of the southeast into the pagan heartlands of the north and west, among kings and their peoples who had never heard it. He evangelised the Ireland that Palladius’s mission had not touched.

The pattern is worth marking because it is characteristically Irish. The faith arrives not by conquest, not imposed by an imperial church on a subdued province, but by trade, by captivity, by the slow capillary action of contact, and then by the conviction of individuals. It comes in sideways, and it makes its home not by smashing the existing order but by moving into it. And moving into it is precisely what it did: the new faith did not so much displace the old beliefs as settle in alongside them, taking over their festivals, their holy places, and their seasonal rhythms rather than abolishing them. The result was less a conquest of one religion by another than a long cohabitation — a theme we will return to, because it runs underneath the whole golden age that follows.

5. The Golden Age, and the Riddle of the Returning Light#

What grew from that grafting was one of the strangest and most luminous things in the early medieval world: a Christianity shaped to fit Ireland, which then turned around and re-lit much of the continent that had supposedly converted it first.

A church with no cities#

Recall the absence at the centre of this chapter. Elsewhere in the former Roman world, the Church organised itself the way Rome had organised everything: around cities. A bishop sat in a city and governed the territory around it — a diocese, mapped onto the old administrative geography. But Ireland had no cities and no such geography. So the Church there grew into the shape of the society it found. The institution that came to dominate was not the diocese but the monastery, founded on land granted by a kindred, headed by an abbot who was often of the founder’s family, mapped onto the kin-structure exactly as everything else in Irish life was. Bishops still existed, because only a bishop could ordain, but real power and prestige gathered around the great abbots. The Irish Church bent itself to the contours of a society with no state — and in doing so became something organisationally unlike anything on the continent.

These monasteries were not the silent, walled retreats of later imagination. The great ones — Clonmacnoise on the Shannon, Glendalough in its valley of two lakes, Kells, Bangor — were among the nearest things to towns that Ireland possessed: crowded settlements of monks, students, craftsmen in metal and stone, farmers working the monastic lands, travellers claiming hospitality, fugitives claiming sanctuary. They were Ireland’s universities, its scriptoria, its art schools, and its first proto-urban centres all at once.

The desert in the rain#

The spirituality that animated them had travelled a long way to get there. Monasticism was an eastern invention, born in the deserts of Egypt and Syria, where the Desert Fathers had fled the cities to seek God through solitude and ferocious self-denial. That ideal moved west — through Gaul, through the island monastery of Lérins and the writings of John Cassian at Marseille — and when it reached Ireland it found, improbably, a perfect home. The Irish took up the eremitical, ascetic strand of eastern monasticism with an intensity the more settled continent never matched. They sought out the wildest, loneliest places to mortify the flesh and strip life to prayer.

The kinship with the Christian East could be startlingly close. Irish monasticism, with its hermits and its harsh bodily discipline and its beehive cells clustered in the wilderness, resembles nothing in Western Christendom so much as the Coptic monasticism of Egypt from which the whole movement had ultimately sprung. There was no direct line — no ship of Egyptian monks landing in Kerry — and the resemblance is best understood as descent from a shared ancestor rather than borrowing between cousins: both drew on the same desert source, and the Irish simply embraced that ascetic strand more wholeheartedly than the settled continent in between. But the family likeness is real and was noticed even in the Middle Ages, and a faint folk-memory of an eastern connection lingered long in Irish tradition. The desert had reached the rain.

There is no purer emblem of this than Skellig Michael, the bare pyramid of rock rising two hundred metres from the Atlantic eight miles off the Kerry coast. There, on a crag where landing is impossible in rough weather and nothing grows easily, monks cut six hundred steps into the cliff and built a cluster of dry-stone beehive huts that have stayed watertight for more than a thousand years. They lived on fish and seabirds and rainwater, in cold and isolation and constant danger, because the hardness was the point. The Egyptian desert had been transplanted to the last rock before the open ocean. To see Skellig is to understand that the Irish did not merely adopt the desert ideal; they pushed it to a place the Desert Fathers themselves might have found extreme.

The love of the word#

And they read, and copied, and wrote. The reverence for the learned word that the filid had always commanded flowed now into a reverence for the Christian book and, with it, for the whole inheritance of Latin letters. Irish monks copied scripture, but they copied the pagan classics too, out of what seems a genuine love of the written word as such.

The supreme survivor of that labour is the Book of Kells, made around the year 800 — a gospel-book containing the four Gospels in Latin, prefaced by canon tables and summaries, written in a fine insular majuscule hand and drowned in ornament: full-page portraits of the evangelists and their symbols, the Virgin and Child, the arrest and temptation of Christ, and above all the great Chi-Rho monogram page, the most lavish single page in any insular manuscript, in which the opening of Christ’s name dissolves into a swarming galaxy of spirals, knotwork, and tiny hidden animals. Tellingly, the book was probably begun on Iona and finished at Kells in County Meath after Viking raids drove the monks from their island — its very making straddles the golden age and the catastrophe that ended it. It survives today in the library of Trinity College Dublin, where a volume lies open under glass, a page turned every few weeks, and remains the thing most visitors to the city queue to see.

In manuscripts like it, the curvilinear genius of La Tène metalwork — those same curling, interlacing patterns once worked in gold for warrior-kings — was now lavished across the opening words of the Gospels. The old aesthetic did not die; it was baptised. In the scriptoria, the deep Celtic past and the Christian present fused into a single, unmistakable visual language.

That fusion had a humbler cousin in stone. Before the monks brought their Latin books, the Irish had devised a writing system of their own: ogham, an alphabet of twenty letters formed from one to five straight strokes notched across the edge of a standing stone. It appears on the historical record in the fourth century and was used, in its monumental form, into the sixth — some four hundred carved stones survive, the great majority of them in the southwest, in Cork and Kerry. The inscriptions are terse, almost always a name in the genitive: of such-and-such, son of such-and-such, marking a grave or a boundary or a claim of descent. The script was probably modelled on the Latin alphabet by someone who knew it — it carries letters for sounds Irish did not use — which makes ogham itself a small monument to the same sideways contact with the Roman world that brought Christianity. It did not survive the triumph of the manuscript. Once monks were writing Irish in Latin letters on vellum, the laborious notching of stones had no future, and ogham dwindled into a curiosity that later scholars half-remembered and antiquarians puzzled over. But for a few centuries it was the first time the Irish language was ever written down at all.

The riddle of the returning light#

Here we reach the paradox that gives this section its title, and it is worth stating plainly because it inverts the natural order of things.

Ireland was the last place Christianity reached — a remote island beyond the edge of the Roman world, converted late and sideways, never imperial, never central. By every expectation it should have been a passive terminus, a place where the faith arrived to settle and go quiet. Instead, within a century or two of its own conversion, Ireland became an exporter. As the Roman order collapsed on the continent and the structures of learning and settled monastic life frayed under invasion and disorder, Irish monks set out eastward — back across the sea to Britain and into the heart of Europe — to found monasteries, copy books, and re-establish the disciplined religious life in lands that had been Christian centuries before Ireland ever was.

They did it under the banner of an idea peculiarly their own: peregrinatio pro Christo, “pilgrimage for Christ” — not pilgrimage to a shrine and home again, but permanent, voluntary exile, the deliberate abandonment of homeland and kin as the supreme ascetic sacrifice. In a society organised entirely around kinship and one’s bonded place within it, to leave forever was the hardest renunciation imaginable, and that is precisely why they chose it.

The roll of these wandering monks is long and consequential. Columba — Colum Cille, “dove of the church” — left Ireland in 563 for the island of Iona off the Scottish coast, and from that windswept base the conversion of Pictish and northern Britain was driven; Iona became a mother-house whose influence reached down into Northumbria and seeded the great monastery of Lindisfarne. Columbanus, the more austere and combative of the two great Columban figures, struck out around 590 into Gaul and beyond, founding monasteries at Luxeuil and, eventually, at Bobbio in northern Italy, scolding kings and even popes in his fierce Latin along the way, and planting Irish monastic discipline deep in the continental ground. Behind and after them came others — Gall, who gave his name to the great Swiss abbey of Sankt Gallen; Aidan, who carried Iona’s Christianity into Northumbria; Killian at Würzburg; Irish scholars who would later ornament the court of Charlemagne. Wherever they went they carried books, learning, the private confessional discipline that would eventually become the norm for the whole Western Church, and the conviction that exile itself was holy.

This is the inversion to sit with. The periphery re-evangelised the centre. The pupil became the teacher. The island that received the faith last, and from others, gave it back to the continent in its hour of collapse — so that a meaningful share of the learning and religious structure of early medieval Europe runs back through these Irish wanderers and the bare island scriptoria that trained them. One can overstate it; the popular slogan that the Irish “saved civilisation” flattens a tangled story in which Byzantium and the continental houses and, later, the Islamic world all preserved and transmitted at least as much. But the core of it is true and remarkable, and it is the first time in this book — it will not be the last — that we watch Ireland’s very marginality become, paradoxically, a kind of power: the edge outlasting the centre, and feeding it. << a few lines on John Scotus >>

The messy reality beneath the gold#

It would be a mistake to leave the golden age gleaming and untroubled, because the Christianity of the ordinary Irish was never the tidy thing the hagiographies imply. Beneath the monastic high culture ran a deep, practical, syncretic religion of daily survival. People prayed against storm and flood and cattle-murrain; they sought out saints as specialists, Brigid for the dairy and the herds, others for this ailment or that danger; they kept the old festival calendar — Imbolc, Beltane, Lughnasa, Samhain — under new Christian names. Holy wells that had almost certainly been sacred long before any Christian blessed them drew pilgrims seeking cures. And the síde, the fairy-folk who were the old gods demoted but not destroyed, kept their hold on the imagination and the landscape: the grass-grown ringforts and ancient mounds became their dwellings, hedged about with taboos no prudent farmer would break. The official faith and this older, earthier layer did not so much do battle as settle into a long cohabitation. The Ireland of the saints was also, always, the Ireland of the holy well and the fairy fort — and any honest account of its Christianity has to hold both at once.

6. The Northmen#

Into this world, at the very end of the eighth century, came the sails.

The first recorded Viking raid on Ireland struck in 795, and the early assaults fell precisely where the wealth and the helplessness coincided: the monasteries. Undefended, rich in metalwork and reliquaries and slaves, sited often on the coast or on navigable rivers, the great religious houses were ideal prey. Iona was hit again and again; Skellig itself, for all its remoteness, was plundered. The annals of the ninth century are studded with burnings and drownings and carryings-off, and the monastic chroniclers — the very men keeping the records — wrote of the Northmen with the bitterness of the robbed.

But raiding is only the first half of the story, and to stop there is to miss the deeper irony. From the middle of the ninth century the Norse began to overwinter, and then to settle, and what they founded changed Ireland permanently: they built towns. Dublin, Waterford, Wexford, Cork, Limerick — every one of Ireland’s significant early cities began as a Viking settlement, a fortified harbour-trading-post planted on the coast by the very people the monks had cursed as destroyers. The Norse brought coinage, urban markets, and Ireland’s first real integration into the trade networks of the wider world, from the Baltic to the Mediterranean.

Pause on the strangeness of that. An island that had never had a city — whose distinctive whole civilisation, we have seen, was built around the absence of the urban and the territorial-administrative — received its first towns not from within and not from the Christian, Latin world to which it was tied, but from pagan sea-raiders out of Scandinavia. The towns were foreign foundations on Irish soil. Within a few generations the Norse of Dublin and the rest were Christianised, intermarried, drawn into Irish dynastic politics as one more set of players, no longer simple aliens. But the pattern had been set, and it is one this book will see again and again: the decisive new thing arriving from outside, planted on the coast, foreign in origin and slowly absorbed — the town as a colonist’s creation in a land whose own genius had been built without it.

The Vikings did not conquer Ireland. They were checked, most famously by the over-king Brian Boru, whose career carried him to a high-kingship of unusual reality and ended at the battle of Clontarf in 1014 — a victory later memory dressed up as the day Ireland threw the heathen into the sea, though the truth, as ever, was messier, with Norse and Irish on both sides of the field. What the Vikings left was not a conquest but a transformation: ports, trade, coin, and a set of coastal towns that would, in time, be exactly the doors through which the next and far more lasting invader would walk.

7. The Eve of Rupture#

By the twelfth century Ireland was, in the long view, both very old and visibly changing — and the changes were quietly dismantling the very things that had made it distinct, just before the catastrophe that would finish the job.

Politically, the island remained a mosaic of kingdoms presided over, in theory, by a high king at Tara — but the high-kingship was a prize fought over rather than an office securely held, and the historians’ phrase for the reality, “high kings with opposition,” tells the story. The great provincial dynasties manoeuvred and warred for a supremacy none could make permanent: the Uí Néill, whose interlinked kindreds dominated the north and midlands and long monopolised the kingship of Tara — it is from their northern expansion, centuries earlier, that the very name of Ulster comes, the old kingdom of the Ulaid having been driven back into the northeastern corner by their advance; the O’Briens of Munster, heirs of Brian Boru; the O’Connors of Connacht in the west; and the MacMurroughs of Leinster in the southeast. It was a vigorous, fragmented, intensely competitive political world, and to its players the rivalries among themselves were the whole of politics. None of them was watching the horizon.

In the Church, meanwhile, a quieter revolution was already underway, and it cut against the Irish grain. A reform movement, driven by men like Malachy of Armagh and crowned by the great synods of the twelfth century, set out to remake the Irish Church on the continental model — to impose, at last, the diocesan and territorial structure that the rest of Europe had always used, to bring Ireland’s idiosyncratic monastic Christianity into line with Rome, and to plant the new continental orders, the Cistercians above all, on Irish ground. From one angle this was renewal. From the angle of this book it is something more poignant: the deliberate dismantling, by the Irish themselves, of the very peculiarity that had made their Church a wonder — the abbot-centred, kin-shaped, gloriously unconventional thing described in section 5 — in favour of conformity with the European norm. The distinctiveness was being filed away from within even before it was overrun from without.

And then, from Leinster, came the spark. Diarmait Mac Murchada, king of Leinster, having abducted the wife of a rival king years before and made enemies enough to match, was driven from his kingdom in 1166 by a coalition of his Irish foes. A king of an Irish túath expelled by other Irish kings was nothing new; what Diarmait did next was. He crossed the sea to seek a patron powerful enough to restore him, and he found one in Henry II, the Norman king of England, who licensed him to recruit among the Norman-Welsh lords of the marches. In 1167 Diarmait returned with the first of them; in 1169 the main body of mailed Norman knights landed on the Wexford coast — landed, fittingly, near one of those Viking-founded coastal towns that had been the island’s doors for three centuries.

They came, at first, as one Irish king’s hired help in one more Irish quarrel. That is how it would have looked to the dynasts still absorbed in their own rivalries. But these were not Norsemen to be absorbed in a few generations, nor mercenaries who would take their cattle and sail home. They were the cutting edge of the most formidable military and administrative culture in western Europe — the people of the castle, the charter, the centralised territorial state, everything Ireland had done without for a thousand years — and behind them stood a king of England who would soon cross over himself to take the island’s overlordship into his own hands.

The world this chapter has described — Celtic, kin-bound, churchless of cities, lit by its strange monastic genius, untaken by Rome and unbroken by the Norse — did not end in 1169. It would prove far tougher and longer-lived than that. But 1169 is the hinge. After it, the central question of Irish history is no longer how this singular society worked on its own terms, but what happened when it collided, permanently and on its own soil, with a neighbour built on the opposite of every principle that had made it. The long argument between Ireland and England begins on a beach in Wexford, with an exiled king and a band of Norman knights, in the autumn of 1169.
